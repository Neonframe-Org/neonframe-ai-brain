# Locale annex: British English

Apply this annex on top of the shared humanizer body when `resolver.humanizer_locales` includes `uk`. This annex outranks the body on spelling, vocabulary, units, money and register.

British English is the default written standard across the United Kingdom and Ireland, and it is the English that Danish, Dutch, German and Nordic clients almost always expect in their English-language material. Treat American forms as errors here, not as stylistic variation.

## British spelling

**-ise, not -ize:** organise, recognise, realise, prioritise, customise, optimise, finalise, categorise, emphasise, summarise, maximise, minimise, visualise, specialise, apologise, standardise. Also the noun forms: organisation, optimisation, categorisation, standardisation. The -ize form is defensible in academic publishing that follows a specific house style, but for business writing use -ise throughout. Sweep for -ize every single time; it is the most common Americanism to survive a draft.

**-yse, not -yze:** analyse, paralyse, catalyse. The noun stays analysis.

**-our, not -or:** colour, honour, favour, behaviour, neighbour, labour, humour, endeavour, flavour, vapour, rumour, harbour, savour. And onwards through favourite, honourable, behavioural, colourful. Note the exceptions that drop the u before certain suffixes: honorary, humorous, laborious, vigorous.

**-re, not -er:** centre, theatre, fibre, metre, litre, calibre, spectre, sombre, lustre. Keep the American form only inside a proper noun registered that way.

**-ence and -ce for nouns:** defence, offence, pretence, licence as the noun and license as the verb, practice as the noun and practise as the verb. The noun and verb split is real and readers notice it: "a licence to trade" but "licensed premises"; "a general practice" but "we practise what we preach".

**Double l before a suffix:** travelled, travelling, cancelled, cancelling, labelled, modelled, fuelled, levelled, marvelled, signalling, counsellor, counselling, jeweller, dialling.

**-ement, not -ment:** judgement, acknowledgement, abridgement. Legal writing prefers judgment for a court's decision; follow that convention inside legal documents and stay consistent within the piece.

**Other standard British forms:**
- programme for a schedule, a broadcast, a course of work, or a funded initiative. Program only for software. This is the sharpest single divergence from Australian usage, where program covers everything
- fulfil, fulfilled, fulfilment. Single l in the base word
- catalogue, dialogue, analogue, monologue. Keep the -ue
- cheque for a written payment order, check for verifying something
- enrol, enrolled, enrolment
- instil, distil, instalment
- storey for a floor of a building, story for a narrative. A three-storey building tells a good story
- towards, afterwards, forwards, backwards, upwards. Keep the s
- learnt and spelt are both fine alongside learned and spelled. Pick one and hold it
- ageing, not aging
- sizeable, likeable, liveable, saleable. Keep the e
- grey, not gray
- whilst is acceptable and reads naturally in British prose, though while is never wrong and is usually cleaner. Amongst and amidst are similarly acceptable and similarly optional
- inquiry for a formal investigation, enquiry for a question. The distinction is still observed in British institutional writing
- sceptical and sceptic, never skeptical
- tyre on a wheel, kerb on a street, axe as a tool, plough in a field, mould on bread, draught for a current of air and draft for an early version
- speciality, not specialty, outside medicine
- aluminium, not aluminum
- Collective nouns take a plural verb when the group acts as individuals: "the board are divided", "the team have agreed". Both forms exist; choose by meaning and stay consistent

When unsure whether a form is British or American, take the longer, older form. British orthography usually kept it.

## Units, dates and money

**Units:** metric is the legal standard and the working default for weight, volume, area, temperature and most measurement. Imperial survivals are genuine and using metric in their place reads wrong:
- Road distance and speed: miles and miles per hour, always. Never kilometres for a British road journey
- Draught beer and milk: pints
- Body weight: stones and pounds in everyday speech, kilograms in clinical writing
- Height: feet and inches in everyday speech, centimetres on official forms
- Land: acres for farmland and development sites, hectares in planning documents
- Commercial property: square feet is still the trade standard for offices and retail, though square metres appear in newer leases. Follow the client's own convention and mark which unit is in use
- Temperature: Celsius. Fahrenheit appears only in tabloid weather copy about heatwaves
- Fuel: litres at the pump, miles per gallon for consumption, which is an inconsistency to reproduce rather than fix

**Dates:** day before month. The full form is D Month YYYY. In numerals, DD/MM/YYYY. Never MM/DD/YYYY. Avoid ordinal suffixes in formal writing: write 9 March, not 9th March or March 9th. Financial quarters are written Q1 to Q4, and half years as H1 and H2.

**Time:** twenty-four hour clock in schedules, timetables and formal agendas. Twelve hour with am and pm in conversational copy. Reference GMT and BST correctly and remember the clocks change, so name the local time and let the reader convert.

**Currency:** pounds sterling. The pound sign precedes the figure with no space, and pence are written as 50p rather than £0.50 in consumer copy. Write large figures consistently: £1.2m or £1.2 million, £4bn or £4 billion. Use commas as thousands separators with a point for decimals. Mark euros and dollars explicitly, and note that Irish clients work in euro even though they read British English.

**Tax:** VAT at 20 per cent is the standard rate. A reduced rate of 5 per cent applies to categories such as domestic fuel and some renovations, and a zero rate applies to most food, children's clothing, books and newspapers. Business to business figures are quoted excluding VAT; consumer prices must be shown including VAT. Say which basis a figure uses every time. Write "per cent" as two words in prose and use the symbol in tables.

**Tax year and reporting:** the personal tax year runs 6 April to 5 April. The corporation tax accounting period follows the company's own year end, so name the period rather than assuming. Self assessment deadlines and Companies House filing dates hang off those year ends. Avoid the American habit of treating the calendar year as the tax year.

**Seasons:** northern hemisphere. Peak retail trade runs from Black Friday through to Christmas in cold weather, the summer holidays empty offices through late July and August, and the new business year effectively restarts in September.

## British business register

British business writing is indirect on the surface and precise underneath. The meaning lives in the understatement. Miss that and the text reads as either brash or naive.

- Understate. "It worked rather well" is high praise. "That went better than expected" reads as a genuine success. Loud claims trigger suspicion, not confidence.
- Soften the surface, keep the substance clear. "Would you mind sending the figures by Thursday" and "I wonder whether we might revisit the timeline" are polite forms of firm requests. Softening the wrapper is expected; leaving the ask vague is not. Every softened sentence must still contain the actual request, the actual date, and the actual number.
- Irony and dry humour are welcome in internal notes, industry writing and client email once a relationship exists. Keep them out of contracts, tenders, regulatory correspondence and anything that might be read aloud in a dispute.
- Criticism arrives by implication. "There may be one or two issues with the third section" means the third section needs rewriting. When writing on a client's behalf, match that calibration rather than escalating it.
- Understand the negatives. "Not bad" means good. "Interesting" can mean wrong. "With respect" precedes disagreement. Use these deliberately or not at all; used carelessly they read as passive.
- Apology functions as social lubricant, not as an admission. "Sorry, could I just check one thing" is standard. Never confuse it with accepting liability in anything contractual.
- Never open with "I hope this email finds you well." A brief weather remark or a reference to the last conversation does the same job without the machine smell. In internal mail, skip the opening entirely.
- Avoid the American motivational register outright. No "let's crush this", no "excited to announce", no "incredible", no "amazing". Enthusiasm is expressed by specificity: say what happened and let the reader be impressed.
- Avoid the American habit of stacking credentials early. British readers prefer relevance first and credentials later, or not at all.
- Titles and honorifics still matter in law, medicine, academia, the civil service, the City and anything royal or charitable. Follow the counterpart's own signature block exactly.
- Sign off with Kind regards, Best wishes, or Many thanks. "Regards" alone reads cool, which is sometimes the intent. Avoid "Sincerely" without "Yours" in front of it.
- Class and regional signalling is live in British writing. Avoid slang that fixes the writer to a particular background unless that is a deliberate part of the voice, and never write regional dialect for colour.
- Where a piece is public facing, check whether the client is Scottish, Welsh, Northern Irish or Irish before writing "British" or "national". Legal systems, education systems and public bodies differ across the nations and getting it wrong is an obvious tell.

## British final sweep

Run these three passes after the shared final check. They catch the errors that slip through most often for this locale.

1. **Search for -ize, -yze, -our as -or, and -er as -re.** Organize, analyze, color, honor, behavior, center, theater, meter. This family is by far the biggest source of leakage, and a single "organize" in a tender document undermines the rest.
2. **Check programme against program.** Programme for schedules, broadcasts, courses and funded initiatives; program only for software. Then check the noun and verb splits: licence and license, practice and practise, defence not defense.
3. **Check date order, the pound sign and the tax basis.** Any MM/DD date will read as a different day, any bare dollar figure needs marking, and any price quoted without saying whether it includes VAT will generate a query. While sweeping, flatten anything that would make a British reader wince at the hype.
